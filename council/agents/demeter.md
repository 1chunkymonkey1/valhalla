    I. Concept:
        Agrivoltaics is the concept of incorporating agriculture and farming with photovoltaic
systems. A common implementation of this system is in the form of placing solar panels over
grazing fields for livestock, or implementing solar photovoltaic arrays in close proximity to
agricultural crops. This system has several advantages and disadvantages in its current form.
Unlike the majority of currently existing renewable and carbon-free energy concepts, it requires
very little new land in order to be developed. Globally, approximately 45% of all land is utilized
for farming and agricultural purposes, with 80% of this agricultural land used for livestock, while
the rest 20% dedicated towards farming plants. With this in mind, since farming and agriculture
are inherently integral components of human infrastructure, the implementation of agrivoltaic
systems allows this land to be used more efficiently than compared to a conventional farm. A
disadvantage of currently existing photovoltaic systems is that by absorbing sunlight, they reduce
the amount of sunlight that plants are able to absorb in the sun, and as such, agrivoltaic systems
are limited to crops which can be grown in the shade, which leaves out the majority of crops. To
alleviate this issue, this concept calls for the attachment of LED fixtures underneath agrivoltaic
systems in order to provide crops with the necessary to grow through the season. While this may
appear counterintuitive, such a system is able to generate net energy as crop plants are inherently
inefficient at absorbing sunlight into photosynthesis to grow, due to their inability to fully absorb
all sunlight that hits them, and so only a small fraction of sunlight energy is necessary for plants
to photosynthesize at an optimal rate. This allows the negative inabilities of agrivoltaic systems
to be overcome, allowing their greater implementation en masse.

3a. Carbon Economics
       Farming infrastructure also has great potential to be incorporated with existing methane
generation systems. Agriculture generates approximately 10% of all global carbon emissions,
and electricity makes up 27%. Out of this 10%, significant portions of agricultural carbon
emissions are a result of the production of fertilizers, the burning of waste crops, and carbon
emissions by livestock. Considering these sources, agrivoltaic methane generation can
significantly address each of these issues. First, one method of addressing carbon emissions from
livestock is through conversion of livestock manure into methane. This tactic is already
implemented in many farms across the world, suggesting that methane generating infrastructure
is already ingrained into the agricultural sector, and thus will not require heavy new
infrastructure in order to be implemented. At the same time, waste crops can also be converted
into methane in the same way as livestock manure, allowing this portion of carbon emissions to
be addressed as a result. Furthermore, it is also beneficial for us to consider the specifics of a
methane generation system. One such method of production would be to react electrically
produced hydrogen with the raw, carbon-rich material found in manure and waste crops. In waste
crop and manure, approximately 45% by weight is elemental oxygen, 45% by weight is
elemental carbon, 6% hydrogen, and 1.5% nitrogen. The remaining components are
approximately 1% by mass or less. Therefore, when we react biomass with hydrogen, the
majority of products should be water, ammonia, and methane. Water and ammonia can be easily
separated from the product due to their significantly lower boiling points, allowing the majority
of product for such a system to be extracted as methane. When we consider this waste ammonia,
while only a small portion of the total product, becomes a significant amount when we consider
the large scales of energy production an agrivoltaic system would require. At the same time,
ammonia is also a prevalent fertilizer used for agricultural purposes, allowing this necessity for
fertilizer to be alleviated through this excess fertilizer. As such, when we consider the total
carbon emissions of agriculture and energy production, an agrivoltaic methane generation system
has the potential to alleviate both, cutting out 37% of all global carbon emissions in the process.

III. Optimizing the System:
        The true benefits of an agrivoltaic methane generation system cannot be fully understood
without understanding how such a system can be optimized and how such an optimized system
would perform. First, let us consider an optimized solar generation system. If an agrivoltaic
methane generation system is to be implemented in its simplest form, this can be done by simply
placing solar panel fixtures on top of a field of crops. While simple, this method has several
disadvantages. First, photovoltaic panels are relatively expensive, especially when we consider
the large amount of land required to cover a farm. At the same time, conventional fixtures are
often immovable and heavy, which can be a negative for use in an agricultural setting. This is
because on many farms, agriculture is often performed through the use of tractors and other
large, industrial machines, and so permanent solar panel fixtures would prevent the use of
industrial farming equipment in such a setting. To alleviate both of these issues, several things
can be done.

1. Optimizing Ease of Use:
       First, to alleviate the issues regarding the use of industrial farming equipment, an
agrivoltaic solar array can be built to be set on top of an E-Z up type system, or a scissor-lift type
mechanism. This way, the entire structure can be collapsed or folded with relative ease, allowing
such a system to be used on industrial agricultural farms. This also has other scalability benefits,
as by connecting multiple sets of solar generators under the same structure, these solar generators
can be built to share the same electrical connectors and systems. Along with this, connecting
these generator systems together in this manner allows this solar system to be modularized with
relative ease for large-scale farm operations, further increasing the feasibility of such a system.
2. Optimizing Costs:
       Secondly, to address the expensive nature of solar photovoltaic panels, several
modifications can be done to reduce costs. First, one quick modification would be to use
concentrated solar photovoltaic arrays rather than simply photovoltaic systems. Concentrated
photovoltaic arrays concentrate the solar energy of the sun onto a small area of solar panels,
allowing a small amount of photovoltaic panels to absorb a large area of sun. When
concentrating this solar energy, this often requires the use of mirrors or fresnel reflector systems,
which can be manufactured far cheaper than traditional solar photovoltaic panels. In practice,
such concentration systems can concentrate solar irradiation somewhere between 50 to 500 times
the intensity of the sun, therefore indicating reductions in costs on these approximate
magnitudes. Furthermore, another implementation that can greatly reduce costs is through the
use of bifacial solar panels. Bifacial solar panels are solar panels which are able to generate
electrical energy on both sides of the solar panel face, and as such, the amount of solar panels
necessary for such a system can be reduced in half. While slightly more expensive than
traditional panels, the slight increase in costs is offset by the significant reductions in the amount
of panels necessary to cover the same amount of area. When adopting these methods to reduce
costs, alternative efficiency benefits can also be realized within such a system. While traditional
solar photovoltaic panels are approximately 25% in their current technological form,
concentrated photovoltaic systems are able to achieve 40% efficiency in modules and 30%
efficiency within systems, indicating a significant increase in power generation between the two
technologies. With all of these changes in mind, an agrivoltaic generation system of this design

would have massive benefits in both costs and power output, even compared to existing solar

       Outside of the panels, however, there are other parts of this system that also cost money.
For instance, the panels and generator system need to rest on top of the EZ-up structure in order
to work, which costs money. One way to reduce costs is to pick a cheap material for this design.
This is favorable, considering the large scales that we will need to cover in order to have a full
agrivoltaic system. Since we need relatively strong strength and long lifetime for the structure,
the possible materials to use are: stainless steel, normal steel, aluminum, plastic, and galvanized
steel. There are other possibilities as well, such as wood or other metals. However, these are
often either too expensive or unable to fulfill the corrosion resistance requirements of such a
system. Out of the chosen materials, the best is probably galvanic steel. Galvanic steel is more
corrosion resistant than normal steel, but less expensive than stainless steel. It is also stronger
than plastic. In terms of its corrosion resistance, galvanic steel still corrodes with time, but can
usually last a couple decades, which is around the same time it takes for a solar generation
system to either go obsolete or break down from degradation, meaning that it will suffice as a
material. Another way to reduce the costs of the structure is to simplify its design. By
minimizing the number of parts, the complexity of the parts, and the size of the parts, this should
reduce both material and construction costs. For instance, galvanized bolts should suffice for
many applications, although depending on the application, stainless steel may be better.
Allowing the panels to have some overhang over the structure should also reduce the size
requirements of the structures themselves. Furthermore, since we discussed the use of an EZ-up
type structure, making each strut/leg/part be longer should reduce the construction costs without
increasing the material costs by a significant value. Any moving parts should also be greatly
simplified, such as the sliding components that allow the EZ-up design to collapse. This should
reduce our costs greatly. Where it is possible to use plastic and it is cheaper, we should do so.
We should also avoid materials that degrade quickly with time such as plastics that degrade to
UV light or rubber and raw steel components. (Note: while more expensive, it may be
worthwhile to consider the use of nylon/teflon spaces between sliding mechanisms, as to reduce
the wear of the system.)

3a. Radiator Panels and Atmospheric Water Generation
       Another more unique method of increasing the efficiency of the panels is to remove the

circulated around the crops themselves, which can benefit from a constant supply of new carbon
dioxide, increasing their growth rate. Alternatively, it may also be possible to mount desiccant
materials underneath the panels. Desiccants, which absorb water from the air, have significant
advantages for their use. By mounting desiccant underneath the panels, they can absorb water
throughout the day, and especially at night, and when the panels heat up, the heat forces the

cool the panels much more effectively, but could also be implemented as a method of collecting

water from the air, reducing the paid water consumption of a farm in general. (Alternatively this
lets the farm take advantage of water that evaporates from growing the plants and recapture it to

3b. Stirling Engines
       If bifacial panels are no longer considered, another unique design element may also be
considered for their use in an agrivoltaics generation system, being the use of Stirling engines.
Stirling engines, a type of heat engine that can convert heat to mechanical work, are a well-
established portion of energy generation. As Stirling engines rely on external heat sources, just
like steam engines and turbines, they are able to run off of external heat sources such as solar
thermal energy, and due to their efficient cycle and relatively smaller size compared to steam
engines and turbines, are able to rival solar panels in many ways. In fact, the world record for
energy generation using solar energy was achieved by Sandia National Laboratory through the
use of a concentrated solar Stirling generation system, at approximately 31.4% efficiency.
However, other considerations make adopting Stirling generators perhaps less favorable. First, as
Stirling engines are mechanical in nature, this means that they require more maintenance and are
more prone to failure compared to traditional solar panels. One technology which provides a
solution is the use of thermoacoustic or linear Stirling generators, which have two great
advantages over existing Stirling engines. First, a linear design removes the crankshaft
components of a Stirling engine, reducing maintenance and mechanical inefficiencies. At the
same time, under the correct design, a linear Stirling generator can be made to generate AC or
DC energy directly that is also in sync with the energy grid frequency, two characteristics that
are often large sources of inefficiency for photovoltaic and other generation systems. Stirling
engines also have more room for inherent growth as a system, as Stirling engines are bound by
the Carnot efficiency, which states that the efficiency of an engine approaches closer and closer
to 100% the higher the temperature of the system, a limit which is only set by material
properties. For the most common metals capable of surviving high temperatures, nickel-based
superalloys, with a working temperature up to 1200 C, this places their maximum Carnot
efficiency at approximately 80%. If we consider the use of high-temperature ceramics, which are
usually more expensive (with a few exceptions), these materials should be able to achieve Carnot
efficiencies of 90% and higher. In contrast, photovoltaic panels are limited to an efficiency of
approximately 68% at the absolute maximum due to the mechanisms of which they operate
                                                                                                   19

under, a value which is also often substantially lower due to practical requirements. This way, a
concentrated Stirling array can produce four times the amount of energy than a contemporary
solar panel array, with far less waste heat generated as well. Depending on the materials used on
their construction (where cost will likely go up with efficiency), a Stirling engine may also be
cheaper to construct per watt of power compared to a photovoltaic panel. Stirling engines, while
requiring more maintenance (although a linear design may render this comparison irrelevant), are
also less prone to degradation over time compared to solar panels, which can generally only last
20 to 30 years before the degradation in efficiency makes them impractical to use, while even the
earliest Stirling engines ever built remain functional today (from the 19th century). When
comparing concentrated photovoltaic to concentrated Stirling generators, Stirling generators are
also less prone to reflection than photovoltaic panels, which could possibly simplify system
construction by reducing the need for sun tracking. The efficiency of a Stirling engine can also
be improved by using vacuum insulation of glass construction to prevent conduction losses to the
atmosphere. When it comes down to it, concentrated solar stirling definitely has potential to
overcome concentrated photovoltaics, which will depend entirely on the industry to develop
reliable, simple, low maintenance, and efficient Stirling engine designs. Stirling engines and
photovoltaic panels can also be used together, with the Solar panels generating energy using
sunlight and then the Stirling engine generating energy using the heat of the panels (although the
efficiency of the Stirling engines may be reduced as photovoltaic panels have a temperature limit
at which they melt/ lose functionality).
       If a Stirling engine does get implemented, this may also allow the system to operate using
thermal batteries by storing concentrated solar energy as heat. At the same time, Stirling engines
run using temperature differences, and so in order to maximize efficiency, it may be even more
efficient to store the thermal energy during the day and then generate the heat at night, when
temperatures are slightly colder. These thermal batteries can also be fluctuated to fit the use
demands of the farm, energy grid, or solar conditions.
       Another consideration (although far less urgent) is the frequency that would be generated
by a Stirling engine. While a singular Stirling engine generates minimal noise, over the course of
a 75-acre farm, the large number of Stirling engines would likely generate an audible noise due
to the oscillating pistons. As such, one way to solve this potential problem is to reduce the
frequency of which the pistons oscillate. This can be done by designing the engines to operate at
                                                                                                        20

a reduced frequency, which can be done by tuning the displacer that is connected to the energy
grid. At the same time, multiple engines can be run against each other such that their operating
frequencies cancel out each other, similar to a double acting piston. Pistons can also be replaced
with flexible and springed diaphragms to reduce mechanical wear.

3c. Fresnel Reflector Design
        We should also consider the design of the fresnel reflectors at this point. The main benefit
of using fresnel reflectors over traditional lenses and reflectors is that they are far lighter and
cheaper to produce. However, there is also not just one type of fresnel reflector. For instance,
most fresnel reflectors are parabolic in shape, which allows the reflectors to have very small
focal points, which is good for systems that need high concentration values, although this also
means that for a parabolic reflector, the angle of the incoming light must be orientated correctly
in order for the correct focal point to be hit. This is why parabolic reflectors (both traditional and
fresnel designs) rely on sun tracking. Alternatively, we can consider a spherical reflector. Since
spheres are omnidirectional in nature (they are geometrically the same no matter what direction
you look at them (in contrast to something like a cube)), this means that light is always reflected
to the same location, so the system can be simplified (and do not need to use sun tracking).
However, spherical reflectors are also less concentrated in nature, which can reduce the amount
of concentration needed to reach high efficiencies. To achieve the maximum benefits of the two,
a mathematical curve can be generated such that the curve reflects a geometry somewhere
between that of a parabolic arc and a sphere, which would allow us to maximize both
                                                                                                       21

concentration and reduce the need for sun tracking. One aspect of a spherical reflector that may
allow us some freedom in doing this is that compared to a 360 degree spherical reflector, our
reflector will only need to have a constant focal point for 180 degrees of incoming light
direction, reducing the requirements of designing such a lens.

3d. Reflector vs. Lens
        Also, there is no specific requirement that states that a reflector should be considered
over a lens concentrator, in which then both should be considered for use. Geometrically, a lens
and a reflector share many similarities, but a lens may have some benefits that a reflector does
not have. For instance, a reflector requires a reflecting element, which is usually made out of
metal. In contrast, a lens does not require a reflecting element, and can be fully constructed out
of plastic. In this case, a lens may be more economical since we can exclude metal as a material
from the lens. The benefits of a hybrid spherical and parabolic lens can also be realized using a
lens. Other differences also affect the lens vs. reflector argument. Using a reflector means that all
of the light underneath the reflector gets blocked up, and so the plants underneath will live in
shade darkness. In contrast, lenses only redirect light that hits the lens from the right direction,
and so a bit of light that is not parallel to solar irradiation will pass through the lens onto the
                                                                                                   22

ground, such as light that is reflected by overhead clouds. This light is usually just reflected away
from the reflector and is not used by the generator in either design. In this regard, the benefits of
having this extra light have to be considered. Having this extra light would allow the plants
underneath to have just a bit more light, but this may also increase the rate of water evaporation,
increasing water consumption. In this case, the mild benefits of the two are likely too small to
outweigh the benefits of using a lens system in the first place, being the cost reductions of using
a lens vs a reflector.
3e. Linear vs Point Concentrator
        Furthermore, we should consider whether we want to use a linear concentrator or a point
concentrator. For comparison, a linear concentrator concentrates light onto a one dimensional
beam, while a point concentrator concentrates light onto a dot (zero dimensions). This is once
again a trade off of complexity and efficiency. Using a linear concentrator, there is one less
dimensional access that needs to be considered when working with light coming from the sun,
especially when working with sun tracking designs, which can greatly increase simplicity. In
contrast, a point concentrator is able to focus the light onto a much smaller spot, which can
increase efficiency since more concentration usually means higher efficiency. However, point
concentrators do not always work well because with the changing location of the sun throughout
the day, the point of highest concentration also shifts. This is, however, the same problem that
we addressed with the parabolic vs. spherical reflector, of which we can probably maximize the
benefits of both using a parabolic/spherical hybrid lens to maximize generation. Point reflectors
also have the other benefit that when using them, a smaller photovoltaic / Stirling engine can be
used, reducing costs.
3f. Lens Geometry
        When we consider the shape of the lens, there is also the consideration of what geometry
the lens should be in order to maximize the requirements of our system. To maximize efficiency,
we want the highest concentration possible, but higher concentration will also mean a bigger
lens. A bigger lens is a heavier lens, so we probably want the lens geometry that will maximize
stiffness (since we do not want to spend too much money on the lenses). Based on how torque
works, this means that we want our lens to have as little offshoot from the supports as possible,
suggesting a circle shape. A circle shape is also good, as in order to stiffen up the lens design, we
will want to place a frame around the lens, and a circle has the lowest perimeter length to area
                                                                                                     23

ratio shape, letting us save material costs, although a curved frame may be more difficult to
manufacture. However, a circle shape leaves lots of gaps in our system, so a hexagonal design
would probably minimize this tradeoff of stiffness and weight. There is, however, another
consideration to be made, being that in order for a solar array to maximize the amount of sunlight
captured, the array components need to mesh together. While a hexagonal arrangement is very
weight and space efficient, it can be difficult to incorporate this into an industrial farm, since
unlike squares, rows of hexagons cannot slide next to each other, and we need our structures to
be collapsible and slide next to each other in order to be collapsed. Thus, perhaps a simple square
geometry is best. (Wow, what a long-winded way to just say we’re going to make our lens
square.) A rectangular shape should also be sufficient. However, if we can somehow design a
lens shape that is 1) hexagonal 2) able to slide next to each other 3) collapsible, such as if the
farmer chooses to organize their crops in a hexagonal fashion, then it would be beneficial to use
a hexagonal lens.
3g. LED Optimization
       The use of LEDs can also be optimized to maximize energy production. During the
winter, solar irradiation is low, but as most cropland is not used for growing during the winter,
all solar energy can be diverted towards generating energy. During the spring, plants are just
germinating, and so a far greater amount of solar irradiation can be diverted towards energy
generation rather than crop lights. During the summer, solar irradiation is maximized, suggesting
that there is much more sunlight than the plants need, allowing photovoltaic systems to be tuned
to this excess sunlight. Finally, in the fall, as crops are harvested, the photovoltaic system can
once again focus on energy production instead of generating LED energy for the plants. Since
plants also do not absorb green light (as indicated by their green color), these LEDs will also not
need to generate green light, reducing energy demands (with exceptions to non-green plants such
as red and purple cabbage).

3h. Short-Term Energy Storage
        When considering energy generation, another inherent element that should be considered
is energy storage. While the main method of mass energy storage for the entirety of the
agrivoltaic system is going to be the methane generation system, it may also be worthy to
consider alternatives to short term energy storage that may be more effective, when considering
shorter storage times and lower storage requirements. For this purpose, our best options can
probably be cut down to electrical batteries, thermal batteries, or chemical batteries. Chemical
batteries in this instance, likely refers to the methane generation system. By incorporating a high
temperature methane fuel cell, the energy of methane can be quickly dispatched back for use on
the farm, such as for powering the LEDs or other farm operations. Lithium-ion batteries are able
to achieve even faster response times, although they are often of high cost and are of lower
energy density. Thermal energy storage can be incorporated with a concentrated solar stirling
system, and is quite reliable and relatively inexpensive, although it requires the use of secondary
heat engines to convert this heat back into electricity. In these ways, perhaps the use of a high
temperature methane fuel cell is the best, given its ease of incorporation, and so to maximize the
energy storage effectiveness of the system, it will likely be necessary for the farm to pre-plan the
use of electricity before hand, just to give a methane fuel cell a few short moments to heat up
before generation. However, a fourth option that can be considered is the use of nickel-iron
batteries. While both nickel-iron batteries and lithium-ion batteries store electrical charge,
nickel-iron batteries are far less expensive, and far more mechanically robust and can last for
decades, whereas lithium-ion batteries degrade within several years and can be chemically
reactive (sometimes in bad conditions they explode). However, nickel-iron batteries are not able
to store as much energy as lithium-ion batteries, requiring the need for a very big array. These
considerations need to be made when picking the right system.

4b. Comparison of Methane Generation Methods
       When discussing energy storage in the form of methane, there is also another important
aspect to consider, which is inherent to any non-cell battery systems. This is the fact that all
processes which convert plant biomass to methane inherently generate oxygen. However, the
energy that is required to produce oxygen often becomes lost, as due to the fact that oxygen can
be extracted easily from the air (20% of the atmosphere is oxygen), there is little value in storing
generated oxygen. Under these considerations, it may appear that this is an inefficiency that is
unavoidable when working with methane generators, although this may not be fully true. First,
some natural gas reactors are able to work with pure oxygen, which increases their combustion
temperature and the efficiency of the system. At the same time, since oxygen is so readily
available in the atmosphere, the atmosphere effectively serves as the storage tank for oxygen
production, suggesting that it is not so much that the energy of the oxygen is lost, but the energy
becomes regained when the methane is burned back. Since the same amount of oxygen enters
and leaves the atmosphere, this energy cannot be considered “wasted” in the same way as waste
heat or waste steam. Furthermore, it is this characteristic that actually benefits methane
generation, as unlike lithium ion batteries, oxygen does not need to be stored with the methane,
allowing methane to have a far higher energy density for energy storage purposes. It is also this
property that makes metal-air batteries so powerful. Furthermore, methane generation is able to
take advantage of the chemical potential energy that is stored in carbon products, whether the
carbon source is trash, manure, or waste crop, so any efficiency losses can be prevented in this
way. When we consider this full cycle of methane production and energy, we actually end up
with a round-trip efficiency of approximately 53.3%, with the source of this low storage
efficiency stemming from each of the steps of the process. As such, I would like to address some
of these steps and how we can increase this storage efficiency. The following process I list below
is only one potential pathway.
                                                                                                  29

       The steps of storing methane energy and converting it back to energy goes as follows:
   1. Hydrogen splitting: water is split into hydrogen and oxygen.
   2. Methanation: hydrogen is reacted with carbon biomass to produce methane
   3. Storage: methane is cooled and stored.
   4. Generation: methane is reacted in a reactor to generate back energy.
       If each step is 90% efficient, these four steps would actually total to a round-trip
efficiency of 65.61%, showing the importance of maximizing each step’s efficiency.
       First, there are several ways hydrogen splitting can be made more efficient. One such
method of increasing electrolysis efficiency is to increase the temperature of which this occurs.
This can be done by recycling the heat generated by various processes throughout the system.
       Secondly, methanation is another step that generates a significant amount of waste heat.
When carbon material reacts with hydrogen, this produces methane, which is an exothermic
process. To reduce the loss of efficiency, this heat should be used to heat the water from the
electrolysis process. Since a significant portion of organic material is also composed of oxygen,
this would react with our generated hydrogen to produce water. Thus, to reduce inefficiencies,
the hot steam that is produced should also be recycled through the system to produce hydrogen.
       Thirdly, when storing methane, methane must first be compressed and cooled. One such
device that can do this is a Stirling cryocooler, which creates a temperature gradient through the
use of a heat pump. This way, the heat generated from such a system can be used to once again
support the electrolysis process, further reducing inefficiencies.
       Finally, the last process is generation, in which natural gas is converted back into energy.
For grid-scale energy generation, this can be difficult to control, as this becomes the jurisdiction
of the power plant and the power plant design, with contemporary power plants having
efficiencies of 60% or higher, when cogeneration is applied (similar to how energy is generated
on the UC Berkeley campus). Other systems, however, such as high temperature fuel cells, may
be able to achieve efficiencies up to 80%, although these have yet to see grid-scale
implementation.
       Since steps 1, 2, and 3 are all going to be performed on-site of an agrivoltaic generation
array, this gives a lot of freedom to raise system efficiency. Energy loss in the form of heat
should be minimized by using high temperature insulation. Systems should also be designed to
work at the highest temperatures possible, as the efficiency of electrolysis increases with
                                                                                                   30

temperature up to 1000 C, at which electrolysis automatically runs itself (practically). This is
another important point to consider, as stainless steel is unable to provide corrosion resistance at
such temperatures. As such, the adoption of ceramic parts should be maximized, which can be
quite expensive. (Alternatively, more exotic alloys can be used, such as titanium and nickel-steel
alloys, although these have many of the same disadvantages). The amount of plumbing should
also be minimized to reduce the surface area of locations where heat can be lost, and minimal
amounts of energy conversion (carbon dioxide to carbon, carbon to methane) should be adopted
to prevent losses between processes.
        There are some other processes that also need to be considered that are not part of the
main generation system that can also significantly influence efficiency. First, between each step
(electrolysis, methanation, and storage), there is likely going to be a small degree of
contamination of foreign material, such as steam leaving with hydrogen, the generation of
ammonia from methanation, or steam freezing up the storage system. To prevent this, simple, yet
high efficient barriers need to be put in place to prevent the mixing of such components. For
instance, even at 1000 C, water can be kept liquid through the use of a pressurized vessel. By
operating under this pressurized system, the water will maintain liquid, preventing steam
production while maintaining the benefits of high temperature electrolysis. The Stirling
cryocooler can also be adopted as means to separate system products through fractional
distillation.
        This becomes a different story when we consider methane generation using carbon
dioxide. The steps for this are:
    1. Carbon dioxide conversion: Carbon dioxide is captured from the air and separated into
        carbon monoxide and oxygen. The oxygen is thrown out.
    2. Hydrogen production: hydrogen is produced from water electrolysis.
    3. Methanation: carbon monoxide is reacted with hydrogen to produce water and methane.
    4. Storage: (same as above).
    5. Generation: (same as above).
        With the introduction of an extra step, this reduces the efficiency of such a system
dramatically. One possible method to maintain high efficiency and simple methane generation
even when using CO2 is to use plasma generation. In such a system, methanation and
electrolysis occur in the same vessel. A positive electrode is placed underwater to produce
                                                                                                   31

oxygen, while the negative electrode is kept above the waterline. Carbon dioxide is also injected
above the waterline. When a high voltage is applied to the negative electrode, the carbon dioxide
forms a plasma arc with the surface of the water, where the carbon monoxide and oxygen
molecules react with the hydrogen to produce methane and water. This method is based off of a
similar novel method of ammonia production that was tested at Rice University, which also used
a plasma electrode on the surface of water to produce ammonia. The benefits of this system is
that the entire process can be performed at room temperature, as at the location where the plasma
arc touches the water, the water is heated to extreme temperature, allowing highly efficient
electrolysis. The efficiency of plasma generation can also be increased by using a dielectric
discharge plate system. Along with this, rather than heating the water, heat can be recycled in the
system by heating the carbon dioxide before entering the water chamber, which reduces the
energy required to split the carbon dioxide molecules. However, a negative of this system is that
it inherently requires the carbon dioxide molecules to be split as well, which can require extra
heat in order to do. Furthermore, distilling carbon dioxide or extracting out carbon dioxide from
the atmosphere is another step in the process that needs to be performed, which can further
reduce system efficiency.
       When considering these two methods of generating methane, the choice to use either
comes down to efficiency vs. flexibility. While the first method is more efficient due to there
being less overall steps, the second method is more flexible, as it can be adopted to run on both
atmospheric carbon dioxide and carbon dioxide generated from burning carbon-rich material (i.e.
farm waste, manure, and trash). However, the second method is less efficient. The best way to
decide which system to use is by considering the available carbon sources for either method. If
enough carbon-rich solids are present such that sufficient amounts of energy storage can be
produced, then the first method should be favored. Otherwise, the second method should be
favored. Even then, other factors may also exist that affect determining the better system. For
instance, the ability for method 2 to be performed without a high temperature pressure tank has
significant benefits, especially in reducing the costs of producing such a system. Perhaps the best
way to evaluate the better system is to construct and test both in the real world.
       One way to get the best of both systems is to use one that adopts a cycle somewhere in
between the two. This can be achieved by implementing plasma generation, but instead of using
carbon dioxide, we use carbon monoxide. This can be done based on the source of carbon in the

system. When carbon is extracted from the atmosphere in the form of carbon dioxide, the plasma
will have to be made of carbon dioxide as well. However, when carbon-rich material is available,
oxygen can be reacted with this material to form carbon monoxide instead, which reacts much
more easily with hydrogen to form methane, reducing energy consumption and inefficiencies.

4c. Liquid vs Gas Fuels
       Jumping on a tangent here, it may also be relevant to mention that a wide number of
methods exist to convert carbon, carbon monoxide, methane, ammonia, and biomass into
                                                                                                    34

hydrocarbons, which include solid, liquid, and gaseous products. This includes the
aforementioned Fischer-Tropsch process, Bergius process, Mobil process, syngas to gasoline
plus, and many others, each with their own benefits and negatives. With so many options, it can
be difficult to pick the right one for a farm, as a balance must be struck between efficiency,
economics, availability, storage, and others. Let us start by tackling the products. Since our
purpose is energy storage, this means hydrogen generation, which also means that solid fuels are
right out the window, since they contain very little hydrogen. (Note: This is actually not
universally true. In fact, the volumetric energy density stored in polyethylene and polystyrene is
actually higher than that of even liquid fuels, and plastics rely on a lot of hydrogen to be
produced, although plastic production can be extremely complex and there is a lack of large scale
infrastructure to convert plastic back into energy (Second side note: Except in the rocket
industry, where plastics are commonly used as solid-rocket fuels). When I say that we can throw
solid fuels out the window immediately, I am referring to solid-fuels such as coal, which consist
mostly of pure carbon and have chemical energy densities by volume higher than anything other
than pure metals.) This leaves gaseous fuels or liquid fuels. Diesel and gasoline have
significantly higher volumetric energy densities compared to liquid natural gas, but are also
slightly less energy dense (by roughly 5 MJ/kg). At the same time, liquid fuels are also easier to
store, as they do not require cryogenic storage and can be stored at room temperature and
pressurization. Removing the need for cryogenic storage means one less process is necessary for
the whole system, meaning that there is one less source of inefficiency in total. Methane,
however, is also a far simpler hydrocarbon compared to liquid fuels, thus making it easier to
produce methane efficiently and with less complex systems. In terms of energy use in the United
States, natural gas and liquid fuels are used at approximately the same rate.
       The source of carbon that we use for our energy storage system may also have a
significant impact on the type of fuel that is generated in the end. If the fuel is generated directly
from carbon, this gives us the greatest flexibility, as the carbon material can be directly
converted to either liquid or gaseous fuels. However, if our carbon source is from carbon
dioxide, since most liquid producing processes require carbon monoxide to start with, this will
require that we convert this carbon dioxide to carbon monoxide first. This extra step may
introduce extra inefficiencies, so when working with carbon dioxide as our carbon source, it may
be better to focus on methane generation, and generate liquid fuel when working with solid
                                                                                                    35

carbon. However, a workaround can probably be found if 1) a method of producing carbon
monoxide from carbon monoxide is found and 2) there is a large enough demand for liquid fuels
that it becomes economical to produce liquid fuel instead of methane.
       Since natural gas and petroleum are used at such similar rates, perhaps the best system for
our agrivoltaic system is for a machine that can generate both. This, however, greatly
complicates storage, but would dramatically improve energy storage capabilities and
compatibility with existing infrastructure. Combined, liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons produce
practically 60% + of all energy in the United States, and with 8% nuclear and 12% renewable,
this significantly puts a dent in applying our product with existing infrastructure. Liquid fuels are
also used in far more industries than natural gas, such as ships, aircraft, ground-vehicles, and
electricity generation, where renewables have yet to have a sufficient replacement. In general,
the Fischer-Tropsch process also generates a gradient of fuel rather than any singular fuel, so in
the process of generating liquid fuel, gaseous hydrocarbons will be generated anyway, making
this process very favorable.
       While difficult, a machine capable of producing both is feasible. The Bergius process can
do both and is the process that has the least number of steps, making it the easiest to make
efficient. However, the other processes have more real-world use, perhaps making it easier to
implement them in the short run. When we consider most of these machines, the main
differences that make one device generate liquid instead of gaseous fuel is the specific catalyst
and temperature used in the reaction process. Tuning these elements however, is not the most
difficult thing. Doing so will likely just have to ultimately be based on controlling the water flow
into the system to control pressure and temperature, along with tuning the rate of hydrogen
production in the form of electrical current into the hydrogen generation system. (Fun side note:
it might also be possible to generate margarine (fake butter) using our machine, as they did
during WWII, but let’s not consider that for now).
       One characteristic of hydrocarbon generation systems that is also strongly in our favor is
the fact that most reactions that convert carbon and hydrogen together are exothermic. This
means that when we generate methane or any other hydrocarbon fuel from carbon-rich materials
available on farms, the energy that is being stored in this fuel is not just the energy of the
agrivoltaic system, but also the potential energy of the carbon-rich material as well. Thus, as a
method of energy storage, it is actually possible to extract more energy from the fuel produced
                                                                                                    36

than the energy that is inputted. Thus, even if the conversion efficiency may be low, as an energy
storage system, generating liquid or gaseous fuels can be done extremely efficiently relative to
the energy supposedly being stored.
       Based on this fact, however, we arrive at another requirement that our system must be
able to fulfill. In order for there to be net energy storage, the chemical potential energy stored in
the final fuel product must be higher than the potential energy stored in our initial carbon-rich
material. (This point is irrelevant if we are generating carbon using carbon dioxide.) Sugars and
hydrocarbons have an energy density around 17 MJ/kg, while wood has an energy density of 18
MJ/kg and ethanol has an energy density of 30 MJ/kg meaning that whatever fuel we generate
needs to have a higher energy density than this.
4d. Biological Methane Generation
       Up to this point, all of our research has been into methane generation via artificial means.
The benefits of this are that by artificially producing methane, we can directly control the
reaction rate, allowing us to produce massive amounts with relatively small machines. However,
as mentioned before, most farms in the United States generate methane biologically, using
anaerobic digesters. In order to comprehensively optimize our product, we should also consider
the use of biological methane production as well. Using electrical energy, energy can be used in
several ways to maximize methane production biologically.
       First, anaerobic digestion tanks can be heated. While anaerobic digestion can be
performed at relatively low temperatures, being able to control temperature means the ability to
maximize the environmental conditions of the digestion tank, improving the rate of methane
production.
       Secondly, hydrogen can be bubbled into the anaerobic digestion tanks. By bubbling
hydrogen into these tanks, this gives bacteria more hydrogen to directly attach to the carbon
molecules, allowing the bacteria to produce more methane without extra effort.
       Other less substantial but still important actions can also be taken to increase methane
production, such as system filtering, agitating the fluid, and others, as similar to with an
aquarium, these methods allowing us to increase the health of the bacteria that produce methane.
       A fundamental issue of methane production via anaerobic means, however, is that when
bacteria digest biological material to produce methane, this often leaves a bunch of digestate
behind. This means that there remains a lot of carbon material that can be used for producing
                                                                                                  37

methane, but the bacteria cannot use it. As such, if we want to maximize methane production, we
cannot rely entirely on anaerobic production to maximize methane production. For a purely
anaerobic system to work, there must be some fundamental changes on the system compared to
existing designs, or an anaerobic system must be used in conjunction with an artificial methane
generator.

5. Optimizing Crop Production:
       As mentioned before, there are several ways to enhance the agricultural aspect of this
system, such as using the ammonia and oxygen generated as byproducts of methane production
to fertilize crops, along with oxygenating water and air around crops to increase crop yield. The
LEDs can also be used to provide artificial lighting to extend the daylight cycle to increase crop
growth. Furthermore, protecting the plants from direct sunlight also reduces water usage by
preventing the rate of evaporation from solar heating. Outside of these already mentioned
methods, the agricultural aspect of this system can also be improved through other ways.
Without the need for solar lighting, several limitations of traditional farming can be removed.
                                                                                                    38

For instance, crops will no longer need to be planted in square grid rows, but instead can be
planted in a way to maximize space efficiency or airflow. A hexagonal growing pattern could
maximize the amount of crops that can be planted on a singular unit area of land, and by
designing the crop field to maximize airflow, this ensures that crops receive enough oxygen and
carbon dioxide to grow, which can often be limiting factors for the growth of plants due to the
low amount of carbon dioxide in the Earth’s atmosphere. When producing methane from organic
matter, significant amounts of ash and other waste also gets produced as well, which can often
contain the trace minerals that plants need to grow. Thus, by using these byproducts to fertilize
the ground, this further reduces farmers’ needs to rely on store-bought fertilizers. This also takes
into account another important aspect of fertilizers, as plants primarily acquire their carbon and
oxygen content from the carbon dioxide in the air and the water they absorb from the soil
respectively. Therefore, by removing the carbon from the waste crop before returning the
remnants to the soil, the plants do not lose nutrients in any way, compared to if this waste crop or
manure was used directly as fertilizer. We can also consider the manner of which a plant is
structured. Most often, plants have multiple layers of leaves when viewed from the top down,
meaning that when sunlight hits plants directly from above, only portions of a crop plant’s total
leafage is actually photosynthesizing at any time. Therefore, by placing the LED light structures
on the sides of the crops rather than the top of the crops, the photosynthetic abilities of crops can
be further maximized, further maximizing the growing abilities of plants. Under these
conditions, plants are no longer encouraged to grow vertically as much as if they were under
direct sunlight, which can benefit crop production, as the growing of structural biomass for
plants can often be very taxing of a plant’s growing abilities, as seen on crops such as apple and
orange trees, where the majority of the plant’s biomass exists in the form of the trunks and
branch structures, rather than the actual consumable components of crop. This way, far more of a
plant’s growing capabilities can be dedicated towards producing the food part of a plant rather
than just the structure of the plant, enabling an increase in food production.
       When examining the growing abilities of crops, what limits crops from being able to
grow is their access to water, carbon dioxide, nitrogen-rich fertilizers, and oxygen. Thus, by
optimizing these aspects of a crop’s growing conditions, significant increases in crop production
can be realized.

7. Safety and Environmental Impact
          Something that is important for all large-scale energy generation structures is that there
needs to be a method to maximize safety and reduce environmental impact. Specifically, there
are many risks that come with any solar generation system.
          First, an inherent risk of any energy generation system is the dangers associated with
electricity. Industry standard insulation should suffice for insulting the electrical output of such a
solar array, suggesting that farmers also need to be trained to properly work with these systems.
          However, a larger concern is the risk associated with concentrated systems, being fire
risks. There are several main points of which a fire can be started for a solar agrivoltaic system.
First, fires can be started from the electrical system, such as wire shorts and the sort. To prevent
this, heavy and strong insulation (of multiple layers) is necessary to prevent fire risks. Secondly,
batteries can also catch on fire, and so these must be provided with the proper coverings and
insulation. The methane generation systems must also be properly handled to prevent natural gas
fires and explosions.
          A critical component that may be more difficult to insulate are the concentrator systems.
Traditional solar concentrators are built in deserts and other areas with little biological material,
                                                                                                     41

preventing fire risks. However, for an agrivoltaic farm, this can be a great risk. Even with
concentrated solar systems, concentrated solar light can often be powerful enough to literally
burn birds and other small flying objects, suggesting this to be a major safety risk. Furthermore,
on an agrivoltaic farm, other fire threats are often present, such as pollen in the air, dry plant
material blown by the wind, and others. Several features can be done to reduce this risk.
       First, farm areas should be kept cool and moist through the use of water sprinkling
systems. This not only reduces dust production, but also makes it more difficult for plant matter
to ignite. However, this cannot be done year round, and can be very wasteful of water if done
constantly.
       Care should also be taken to prevent flammable material from entering the region of
concentrated light of the plants. Whether for a Stirling generator or a concentrator photovoltaic
panel, this region makes it very easy for material to get burned, a significant risk. To prevent
this, the path of the concentrated light should be kept away from the ground as to prevent the
crops from being burnt. Transparent windows may also help to reduce this risk. By covering the
hottest areas with transparent windows (and maybe vacuum insulation in the form of a glass
wall), this will prevent debris from entering the areas of highest light concentration. At the same
time, focus should also be put to reduce the amount of horizontal surfaces where flammable
material can rest, such as leaves. Screens and barriers can also be used to prevent dust from
entering the concentrated beam. Reducing the volume of the beam of concentration will also
greatly reduce the risk of fire. This can be done by angling the lens to have a focal point very
close to the lens surface, or by reducing the amount of concentration. If done properly, the fire
risk should be minimized in operation. (Note: This is also a point that shows why a Stirling
generator may be favorable to a photovoltaic panel. In order to concentrate light extremely close
to the fresnel lens panel, the lensed light needs to be extremely close to being parallel to the lens.
As such, at the points where the light contacts the photovoltaic panel, this makes reflection
extremely likely, unless a curved photovoltaic plate is used, which is expensive. In contrast, a
Stirling engine can be manufactured curved quite easily (since pistons are inherently cylindrical),
and they can be constructed out of materials that absorb light much better, allowing for
effectiveness at significant solar concentration compared to a Stirling engine, while maintaining
fire safety. (In theory we can alleviate this lens issue by just attaching a secondary lens to convert
the light back to parallel status, although this once again introduces another part to the system)).
                                                                                                       42

        Another way to reduce the region of maximum solar concentration is to place the
generator in the center of the lens. Due to the nature of lens structures, it is possible for the point
of maximum concentration of a lens to be placed in various locations underneath the lens, giving
us great flexibility in picking a point. As such, we can take advantage of this and place the point
of maximum concentration directly underneath the center of the lens. We can think about this
because of the geometric nature of a concentration lens. The closer we are to the point of
maximum concentration, the greater the light concentration in that area. To visualize this, we can
draw flux lines from each point on the surface of the lens towards the point of maximum
concentration. The density of these flux lines is the greatest at the point of concentration, hence
that is why it is the point of concentration. Thus, reducing the region of maximum concentration
means that we need to prevent the flux lines from overlapping as much as possible. The best way
to do this is to reduce the length of the flux lines. One way to imagine this is like hairy hair. The
hair is the flux lines. Someone with shorter hair is less likely to get tangled hair. Therefore, the
point where all of the flux lines will be the shortest is the center of the lens. (I have not fully
performed a mathematical analysis of these conditions but my intuition tells me this is correct.)
Assuming this is true, by placing the generator in the center of the lens, we can minimize the
region of maximum concentration of the system.

construction project has large environmental impacts. For these farms, the loss of natural

farms that are close to highways, this may be good for people driving at night. Realistically, the
net environmental impact will likely be the same as the combined effects of both a solar farm
and a food farm together.

IV. Costs and Economics


    I. Concept:
        Agrivoltaics is the concept of incorporating agriculture and farming with photovoltaic
systems. A common implementation of this system is in the form of placing solar panels over
grazing fields for livestock, or implementing solar photovoltaic arrays in close proximity to
agricultural crops. This system has several advantages and disadvantages in its current form.
Unlike the majority of currently existing renewable and carbon-free energy concepts, it requires
very little new land in order to be developed. Globally, approximately 45% of all land is utilized
for farming and agricultural purposes, with 80% of this agricultural land used for livestock, while
the rest 20% dedicated towards farming plants. With this in mind, since farming and agriculture
are inherently integral components of human infrastructure, the implementation of agrivoltaic
systems allows this land to be used more efficiently than compared to a conventional farm. A
disadvantage of currently existing photovoltaic systems is that by absorbing sunlight, they reduce
the amount of sunlight that plants are able to absorb in the sun, and as such, agrivoltaic systems
are limited to crops which can be grown in the shade, which leaves out the majority of crops. To
alleviate this issue, this concept calls for the attachment of LED fixtures underneath agrivoltaic
systems in order to provide crops with the necessary to grow through the season. While this may
appear counterintuitive, such a system is able to generate net energy as crop plants are inherently
inefficient at absorbing sunlight into photosynthesis to grow, due to their inability to fully absorb
all sunlight that hits them, and so only a small fraction of sunlight energy is necessary for plants
to photosynthesize at an optimal rate. This allows the negative inabilities of agrivoltaic systems
to be overcome, allowing their greater implementation en masse.

III. Optimizing the System:
        The true benefits of an agrivoltaic methane generation system cannot be fully understood
without understanding how such a system can be optimized and how such an optimized system
would perform. First, let us consider an optimized solar generation system. If an agrivoltaic
methane generation system is to be implemented in its simplest form, this can be done by simply
placing solar panel fixtures on top of a field of crops. While simple, this method has several
disadvantages. First, photovoltaic panels are relatively expensive, especially when we consider
the large amount of land required to cover a farm. At the same time, conventional fixtures are
often immovable and heavy, which can be a negative for use in an agricultural setting. This is
because on many farms, agriculture is often performed through the use of tractors and other
large, industrial machines, and so permanent solar panel fixtures would prevent the use of
industrial farming equipment in such a setting. To alleviate both of these issues, several things
can be done.

1. Optimizing Ease of Use:
       First, to alleviate the issues regarding the use of industrial farming equipment, an
agrivoltaic solar array can be built to be set on top of an E-Z up type system, or a scissor-lift type
mechanism. This way, the entire structure can be collapsed or folded with relative ease, allowing
such a system to be used on industrial agricultural farms. This also has other scalability benefits,
as by connecting multiple sets of solar generators under the same structure, these solar generators
can be built to share the same electrical connectors and systems. Along with this, connecting
these generator systems together in this manner allows this solar system to be modularized with
relative ease for large-scale farm operations, further increasing the feasibility of such a system.
2. Optimizing Costs:
       Secondly, to address the expensive nature of solar photovoltaic panels, several
modifications can be done to reduce costs. First, one quick modification would be to use
concentrated solar photovoltaic arrays rather than simply photovoltaic systems. Concentrated
photovoltaic arrays concentrate the solar energy of the sun onto a small area of solar panels,
allowing a small amount of photovoltaic panels to absorb a large area of sun. When
concentrating this solar energy, this often requires the use of mirrors or fresnel reflector systems,
which can be manufactured far cheaper than traditional solar photovoltaic panels. In practice,
such concentration systems can concentrate solar irradiation somewhere between 50 to 500 times
the intensity of the sun, therefore indicating reductions in costs on these approximate
magnitudes. Furthermore, another implementation that can greatly reduce costs is through the
use of bifacial solar panels. Bifacial solar panels are solar panels which are able to generate
electrical energy on both sides of the solar panel face, and as such, the amount of solar panels
necessary for such a system can be reduced in half. While slightly more expensive than
traditional panels, the slight increase in costs is offset by the significant reductions in the amount
of panels necessary to cover the same amount of area. When adopting these methods to reduce
costs, alternative efficiency benefits can also be realized within such a system. While traditional
solar photovoltaic panels are approximately 25% in their current technological form,
concentrated photovoltaic systems are able to achieve 40% efficiency in modules and 30%
efficiency within systems, indicating a significant increase in power generation between the two
technologies. With all of these changes in mind, an agrivoltaic generation system of this design

would have massive benefits in both costs and power output, even compared to existing solar
panel farms of a similar design.
       Outside of the panels, however, there are other parts of this system that also cost money.
For instance, the panels and generator system need to rest on top of the EZ-up structure in order
to work, which costs money. One way to reduce costs is to pick a cheap material for this design.
This is favorable, considering the large scales that we will need to cover in order to have a full
agrivoltaic system. Since we need relatively strong strength and long lifetime for the structure,
the possible materials to use are: stainless steel, normal steel, aluminum, plastic, and galvanized
steel. There are other possibilities as well, such as wood or other metals. However, these are
often either too expensive or unable to fulfill the corrosion resistance requirements of such a
system. Out of the chosen materials, the best is probably galvanic steel. Galvanic steel is more
corrosion resistant than normal steel, but less expensive than stainless steel. It is also stronger
than plastic. In terms of its corrosion resistance, galvanic steel still corrodes with time, but can
usually last a couple decades, which is around the same time it takes for a solar generation
system to either go obsolete or break down from degradation, meaning that it will suffice as a
material. Another way to reduce the costs of the structure is to simplify its design. By
minimizing the number of parts, the complexity of the parts, and the size of the parts, this should
reduce both material and construction costs. For instance, galvanized bolts should suffice for
many applications, although depending on the application, stainless steel may be better.
Allowing the panels to have some overhang over the structure should also reduce the size
requirements of the structures themselves. Furthermore, since we discussed the use of an EZ-up
type structure, making each strut/leg/part be longer should reduce the construction costs without
increasing the material costs by a significant value. Any moving parts should also be greatly
simplified, such as the sliding components that allow the EZ-up design to collapse. This should
reduce our costs greatly. Where it is possible to use plastic and it is cheaper, we should do so.
We should also avoid materials that degrade quickly with time such as plastics that degrade to
UV light or rubber and raw steel components. (Note: while more expensive, it may be
worthwhile to consider the use of nylon/teflon spaces between sliding mechanisms, as to reduce
the wear of the system.)

3a. Radiator Panels and Atmospheric Water Generation
       Another more unique method of increasing the efficiency of the panels is to remove the
bifacial panels altogether and simply use single faced, concentrated solar panels. The benefit of
this is that this allows the concentrated solar panels to be mounted onto the reflectors
themselves, and thus the panels can be used as a sort of radiator to radiate away the heat of the
panels, thus increasing their energy generation efficiency. At the same time, by angling the
panels in the correct manner, it may also be possible to create a thermal updraft using the heat of
the radiating panels, allowing for air to be circulated underneath the panels to raise system
efficiency. Having an updraft in such a system will also allow fresh air to continuously be
circulated around the crops themselves, which can benefit from a constant supply of new carbon
dioxide, increasing their growth rate. Alternatively, it may also be possible to mount desiccant
materials underneath the panels. Desiccants, which absorb water from the air, have significant
advantages for their use. By mounting desiccant underneath the panels, they can absorb water
throughout the day, and especially at night, and when the panels heat up, the heat forces the
desiccant materials to release the water that they have absorbed. This process would not only
cool the panels much more effectively, but could also be implemented as a method of collecting

water from the air, reducing the paid water consumption of a farm in general. (Alternatively this
lets the farm take advantage of water that evaporates from growing the plants and recapture it to
cool the panels).
3b. Stirling Engines
       If bifacial panels are no longer considered, another unique design element may also be
considered for their use in an agrivoltaics generation system, being the use of Stirling engines.
Stirling engines, a type of heat engine that can convert heat to mechanical work, are a well-
established portion of energy generation. As Stirling engines rely on external heat sources, just
like steam engines and turbines, they are able to run off of external heat sources such as solar
thermal energy, and due to their efficient cycle and relatively smaller size compared to steam
engines and turbines, are able to rival solar panels in many ways. In fact, the world record for
energy generation using solar energy was achieved by Sandia National Laboratory through the
use of a concentrated solar Stirling generation system, at approximately 31.4% efficiency.
However, other considerations make adopting Stirling generators perhaps less favorable. First, as
Stirling engines are mechanical in nature, this means that they require more maintenance and are
more prone to failure compared to traditional solar panels. One technology which provides a
solution is the use of thermoacoustic or linear Stirling generators, which have two great
advantages over existing Stirling engines. First, a linear design removes the crankshaft
components of a Stirling engine, reducing maintenance and mechanical inefficiencies. At the
same time, under the correct design, a linear Stirling generator can be made to generate AC or
DC energy directly that is also in sync with the energy grid frequency, two characteristics that
are often large sources of inefficiency for photovoltaic and other generation systems. Stirling
engines also have more room for inherent growth as a system, as Stirling engines are bound by
the Carnot efficiency, which states that the efficiency of an engine approaches closer and closer
to 100% the higher the temperature of the system, a limit which is only set by material
properties. For the most common metals capable of surviving high temperatures, nickel-based
superalloys, with a working temperature up to 1200 C, this places their maximum Carnot
efficiency at approximately 80%. If we consider the use of high-temperature ceramics, which are
usually more expensive (with a few exceptions), these materials should be able to achieve Carnot
efficiencies of 90% and higher. In contrast, photovoltaic panels are limited to an efficiency of
approximately 68% at the absolute maximum due to the mechanisms of which they operate
                                                                                                   19

under, a value which is also often substantially lower due to practical requirements. This way, a
concentrated Stirling array can produce four times the amount of energy than a contemporary
solar panel array, with far less waste heat generated as well. Depending on the materials used on
their construction (where cost will likely go up with efficiency), a Stirling engine may also be
cheaper to construct per watt of power compared to a photovoltaic panel. Stirling engines, while
requiring more maintenance (although a linear design may render this comparison irrelevant), are
also less prone to degradation over time compared to solar panels, which can generally only last
20 to 30 years before the degradation in efficiency makes them impractical to use, while even the
earliest Stirling engines ever built remain functional today (from the 19th century). When
comparing concentrated photovoltaic to concentrated Stirling generators, Stirling generators are
also less prone to reflection than photovoltaic panels, which could possibly simplify system
construction by reducing the need for sun tracking. The efficiency of a Stirling engine can also
be improved by using vacuum insulation of glass construction to prevent conduction losses to the
atmosphere. When it comes down to it, concentrated solar stirling definitely has potential to
overcome concentrated photovoltaics, which will depend entirely on the industry to develop
reliable, simple, low maintenance, and efficient Stirling engine designs. Stirling engines and
photovoltaic panels can also be used together, with the Solar panels generating energy using
sunlight and then the Stirling engine generating energy using the heat of the panels (although the
efficiency of the Stirling engines may be reduced as photovoltaic panels have a temperature limit
at which they melt/ lose functionality).
       If a Stirling engine does get implemented, this may also allow the system to operate using
thermal batteries by storing concentrated solar energy as heat. At the same time, Stirling engines
run using temperature differences, and so in order to maximize efficiency, it may be even more
efficient to store the thermal energy during the day and then generate the heat at night, when
temperatures are slightly colder. These thermal batteries can also be fluctuated to fit the use
demands of the farm, energy grid, or solar conditions.
       Another consideration (although far less urgent) is the frequency that would be generated
by a Stirling engine. While a singular Stirling engine generates minimal noise, over the course of
a 75-acre farm, the large number of Stirling engines would likely generate an audible noise due
to the oscillating pistons. As such, one way to solve this potential problem is to reduce the
frequency of which the pistons oscillate. This can be done by designing the engines to operate at

a reduced frequency, which can be done by tuning the displacer that is connected to the energy
grid. At the same time, multiple engines can be run against each other such that their operating
frequencies cancel out each other, similar to a double acting piston. Pistons can also be replaced
with flexible and springed diaphragms to reduce mechanical wear.

3c. Fresnel Reflector Design
        We should also consider the design of the fresnel reflectors at this point. The main benefit
of using fresnel reflectors over traditional lenses and reflectors is that they are far lighter and
cheaper to produce. However, there is also not just one type of fresnel reflector. For instance,
most fresnel reflectors are parabolic in shape, which allows the reflectors to have very small
focal points, which is good for systems that need high concentration values, although this also
means that for a parabolic reflector, the angle of the incoming light must be orientated correctly
in order for the correct focal point to be hit. This is why parabolic reflectors (both traditional and
fresnel designs) rely on sun tracking. Alternatively, we can consider a spherical reflector. Since
spheres are omnidirectional in nature (they are geometrically the same no matter what direction
you look at them (in contrast to something like a cube)), this means that light is always reflected
to the same location, so the system can be simplified (and do not need to use sun tracking).
However, spherical reflectors are also less concentrated in nature, which can reduce the amount
of concentration needed to reach high efficiencies. To achieve the maximum benefits of the two,
a mathematical curve can be generated such that the curve reflects a geometry somewhere
between that of a parabolic arc and a sphere, which would allow us to maximize both
                                                                                                       21

concentration and reduce the need for sun tracking. One aspect of a spherical reflector that may
allow us some freedom in doing this is that compared to a 360 degree spherical reflector, our
reflector will only need to have a constant focal point for 180 degrees of incoming light
direction, reducing the requirements of designing such a lens.

3d. Reflector vs. Lens
        Also, there is no specific requirement that states that a reflector should be considered
over a lens concentrator, in which then both should be considered for use. Geometrically, a lens
and a reflector share many similarities, but a lens may have some benefits that a reflector does
not have. For instance, a reflector requires a reflecting element, which is usually made out of
metal. In contrast, a lens does not require a reflecting element, and can be fully constructed out
of plastic. In this case, a lens may be more economical since we can exclude metal as a material
from the lens. The benefits of a hybrid spherical and parabolic lens can also be realized using a
lens. Other differences also affect the lens vs. reflector argument. Using a reflector means that all
of the light underneath the reflector gets blocked up, and so the plants underneath will live in
shade darkness. In contrast, lenses only redirect light that hits the lens from the right direction,
and so a bit of light that is not parallel to solar irradiation will pass through the lens onto the
                                                                                                   22

ground, such as light that is reflected by overhead clouds. This light is usually just reflected away
from the reflector and is not used by the generator in either design. In this regard, the benefits of
having this extra light have to be considered. Having this extra light would allow the plants
underneath to have just a bit more light, but this may also increase the rate of water evaporation,
increasing water consumption. In this case, the mild benefits of the two are likely too small to
outweigh the benefits of using a lens system in the first place, being the cost reductions of using
a lens vs a reflector.
3e. Linear vs Point Concentrator
        Furthermore, we should consider whether we want to use a linear concentrator or a point
concentrator. For comparison, a linear concentrator concentrates light onto a one dimensional
beam, while a point concentrator concentrates light onto a dot (zero dimensions). This is once
again a trade off of complexity and efficiency. Using a linear concentrator, there is one less
dimensional access that needs to be considered when working with light coming from the sun,
especially when working with sun tracking designs, which can greatly increase simplicity. In
contrast, a point concentrator is able to focus the light onto a much smaller spot, which can
increase efficiency since more concentration usually means higher efficiency. However, point
concentrators do not always work well because with the changing location of the sun throughout
the day, the point of highest concentration also shifts. This is, however, the same problem that
we addressed with the parabolic vs. spherical reflector, of which we can probably maximize the
benefits of both using a parabolic/spherical hybrid lens to maximize generation. Point reflectors
also have the other benefit that when using them, a smaller photovoltaic / Stirling engine can be
used, reducing costs.
